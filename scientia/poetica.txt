== poetry | the-art ==
Poetry is language made to carry more than its plain sense — through sound, rhythm,
image and compression, saying much in little and meaning more than it says. Where
prose explains, poetry enacts: it works by the music of the line as much as by the
meaning of the words, and by the image that shows rather than tells. Its oldest
root is song and memory — metre and rhyme were mnemonics before they were
ornaments. Read it aloud; a poem that is only on the page is half dead.

== the-modes | kinds-of-poetry ==
The great kinds, by what they do. Lyric: short, first-person, a single speaker's
feeling or thought in a moment (the ode, elegy, sonnet, song) — the bulk of what we
now call "poems." Narrative: it tells a story (the epic, the ballad, the romance).
Dramatic: spoken by a character not the poet (the dramatic monologue, verse drama).
Didactic: it means to teach. These blur — an epic holds lyric moments — but naming a
poem's mode tells you what it is trying to be, and so how to read it.

== metre | scansion ==
Metre is the measured beat of a line, counted in English by stressed and unstressed
syllables grouped into "feet." The common feet: the iamb (da-DUM, as "be-LONG"),
the commonest in English; the trochee (DUM-da, "GAR-den"); the anapest
(da-da-DUM, "in-ter-RUPT"); the dactyl (DUM-da-da, "TEN-der-ly"); the spondee
(DUM-DUM, two stresses). To "scan" a line is to mark its beats and name the pattern.
The feel follows the foot: iambs walk, trochees fall, anapests gallop.

== the-line-lengths ==
A metre is named by its foot and the count of feet per line: monometer (one),
dimeter (two), trimeter (three), tetrameter (four), pentameter (five), hexameter
(six), heptameter (seven). Iambic pentameter — five iambs, ten syllables — is the
grand line of English verse, of Shakespeare and Milton, close to the natural rhythm
of English speech. The classical epic ran in dactylic hexameter (Homer, Virgil).
Perfect regularity deadens; the living line plays against its metre, substituting a
foot to catch the ear.

== rhyme | the-schemes ==
Rhyme chimes the ends (or elsewhere) of lines by sound. Full rhyme matches the
stressed vowel and all after (moon/June); slant or half rhyme glances off it
(moon/mourn), a subtler modern favourite; eye rhyme only looks alike (love/move).
A rhyme scheme is charted by letters — a couplet aa, alternating abab, enclosed
abba. Rhyme can also fall inside a line (internal) or at the start (head). Its
work is music and memory and the click of closure; overdone it jingles, which is
why much modern verse leaves it or hides it.

== poetic-devices ==
The close-work of the art. Of sound: alliteration (repeated opening consonants),
assonance (repeated vowels), consonance (repeated consonants), onomatopoeia (sound
echoing sense). Of image: metaphor (one thing as another), simile (as/like),
personification, symbol, imagery (the sensory picture). Of the line: enjambment
(the sentence runs over the line-end, pulling the reader on), the end-stopped line
(it rests), caesura (a pause within the line). In the sonnet and elsewhere, the
volta — the "turn," where the poem pivots in thought.

== the-sonnet ==
Fourteen lines, usually iambic pentameter, the most enduring fixed form in English.
Two main builds. The Petrarchan (Italian): an octave (abbaabba) that sets a
problem, then a sestet (cde cde or so) that answers it, the volta falling at line
nine. The Shakespearean (English): three quatrains and a closing couplet
(abab cdcd efef gg), the argument developed through the quatrains and clinched —
often with a twist — in the couplet. The form's tight room is its power: it forces
the thought to turn and to land.

== the-villanelle ==
Nineteen lines on two rhymes and two refrains: five tercets (aba) and a closing
quatrain (abaa). The first and third lines of the opening stanza return, alternately,
as the last line of each following tercet, and both close the final quatrain
together. The circling refrains make it an instrument for obsession, grief and the
unshakeable — Dylan Thomas's "Do not go gentle into that good night" and Bishop's
"One Art" are the famous proofs. Hard to write without strain; unforgettable when it
works.

== the-sestina ==
Thirty-nine lines, unrhymed, turning on six end-words instead of rhyme. Six stanzas
of six lines repeat those same six words as line-endings, in a fixed, spiralling
order (each stanza's last word begins the next), and a three-line envoi uses all six
again, two per line. The pattern (615243, rolled each time) is a machine for
obsessive return without the chime of rhyme. Demanding, strange, and oddly hypnotic;
the recurring words gather new weight each time they come round.

== the-haiku-and-tanka ==
Japanese short forms of great compression. The haiku: three phrases, in Japanese
counted as five–seven–five "on" (sound-units, not quite English syllables), holding
a single sharp image, traditionally with a season-word (kigo) and a cutting pause
(kireji) that juxtaposes two things. English haiku keep the spirit — brevity, image,
the turn — more than the strict count. The tanka adds two more seven-count phrases
(5-7-5-7-7), room for a breath of feeling after the image. Show the thing; let the
feeling arise uncaptioned.

== the-ghazal ==
An ancient form of Arabic root, perfected in Persian and Urdu (Rumi, Hafiz, Ghalib):
five or more couplets, each a self-contained thought, so the poem is a string of
gems rather than one argument. A refrain word (radif) ends both lines of the first
couplet and the second line of every couplet after, often with a rhyme (qafia) just
before it. Traditionally the poet signs a name in the last couplet. Its themes run to
love, longing and the divine, the two often one. Each couplet can stand alone; the
whole is bound by sound and mood, not plot.

== the-ode-and-elegy ==
Two of the great lyric occasions. The ode is a poem of praise and serious address,
lofty in tone — Pindar's public, triumphal odes; Horace's quieter ones; the English
Romantics' meditations on a thing or a state (Keats's "to a Nightingale," "on a
Grecian Urn"). The elegy is a poem of mourning and lament for the dead, moving
classically from grief through praise to some consolation (Milton's "Lycidas," Gray's
"Elegy in a Country Churchyard"). One lifts up; the other lays to rest.

== the-ballad ==
The storytelling song of the folk tradition, made to be sung and remembered. The
ballad stanza: four lines, alternating iambic tetrameter and trimeter, rhyming abcb
(or abab). It tells a tale — often of love, death, betrayal or the uncanny — plainly
and by leaps, with a refrain, repetition, and dialogue, trusting the listener to fill
the gaps ("incremental repetition" builds dread). From "Sir Patrick Spens" to
Coleridge's deliberate "Rime of the Ancient Mariner." Narrative, musical, memorable.

== blank-verse-and-free-verse ==
Two unrhymed modes, often confused. Blank verse is unrhymed iambic pentameter — it
keeps the metre and drops the rhyme, and it is the staple of English verse drama and
epic (Shakespeare's plays, Milton's "Paradise Lost"), near enough to speech to carry
long thought. Free verse keeps neither metre nor rhyme, finding its rhythm in the
phrase, the line-break and the cadence of natural speech (Whitman, and most modern
poetry). Free verse is not formless — its freedom is a harder discipline, since every
line-break must be chosen, not counted.

== lesser-fixed-forms ==
A handful more worth knowing. Terza rima: interlocking tercets, aba bcb cdc..., the
chain Dante built the Commedia on. The pantoum (Malay root): quatrains in which the
second and fourth line of each stanza become the first and third of the next, so the
poem steps forward by always looking back. The limerick: five lines, aabba,
anapestic and comic. The couplet (two rhymed lines, the heroic couplet in
pentameter) and the quatrain (four lines) are the common building-blocks. The
acrostic spells a word down its initial letters; concrete poetry shapes the words on
the page into the thing they name.
