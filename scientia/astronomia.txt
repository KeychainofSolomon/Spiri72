== saturn | saturnus | cronos ==
Saturn — in truth the sixth planet from the Sun and the farthest the eye alone
can follow, a great ball of gas ringed with ice and stone, near ten times
Earth's distance from the Sun, circling once in about 29.5 years. To the old
astronomy it was the highest and slowest of the seven wandering stars. The
tradition reads it cold and dry, the greater malefic: of lead, age, boundaries,
bone and stone, patience and melancholy. Its day Saturday, its metal lead; ruler
of Capricorn and Aquarius.

== jupiter | iuppiter | jove | zeus ==
Jupiter — the fifth planet and by far the largest, a gas giant that could hold
all the others within it, girt by its four great moons (Galileo's, 1610) and the
centuries-old storm of the Great Red Spot; it rounds the Sun in about 11.9 years.
The tradition reads it temperate and warm, the greater benefic: of tin, law,
increase, mercy, princes and religion. Its day Thursday, its metal tin; ruler of
Sagittarius and Pisces.

== mars | mars-planet | ares ==
Mars — the fourth planet, red from the rust (iron oxide) in its dust, cold and
near-airless, with two small moons (Phobos and Deimos) and the tallest volcano
known; it circles the Sun in about 1.9 years. The tradition reads it hot and dry,
the lesser malefic: of iron, war, fire, fever, the knife and sudden anger. Its
day Tuesday, its metal iron; ruler of Aries and Scorpio.

== sun | sol | helios ==
The Sun — not a planet but the star at the centre, a sphere of hot gas turning
hydrogen to helium by fusion in its core, about 93 million miles (one
astronomical unit) off, holding the whole system in its gravity. The old
astronomy set it among the seven and had it circle the Earth; Copernicus (1543)
put it rightly at the centre. The tradition reads it hot and life-giving: of
gold, kings, the heart, honour. Its day Sunday, its metal gold; ruler of Leo.

== venus | venus-planet | aphrodite ==
Venus — the second planet, nearest to us and closest to Earth in size, wrapped in
thick cloud that makes it the brightest star-like light and, by a runaway
greenhouse of carbon dioxide, the hottest surface of any planet. As an inner
planet it never strays far from the Sun, showing as morning or evening star. The
tradition reads it temperate and moist, the lesser benefic: of copper, love,
music, concord. Its day Friday, its metal copper; ruler of Taurus and Libra.

== mercury | mercurius | hermes ==
Mercury — the first planet, smallest and swiftest, closest to the Sun, airless
and burnt by day and frozen by night, rounding the Sun in only about 88 days.
Like Venus it keeps near the Sun and is glimpsed low at dawn or dusk. The
tradition reads it swift and changeable, taking the nature of what it joins: of
quicksilver, speech, trade, letters, cunning and wit. Its day Wednesday, its
metal quicksilver; ruler of Gemini and Virgo.

== moon | luna | selene ==
The Moon — Earth's own satellite, a quarter our width and some 384,000 km off,
shining by borrowed sunlight; it keeps one face always toward us and raises the
sea's tides by its pull. It waxes and wanes through its phases in about 29.5 days
(the synodic month) and is reckoned by its "age" in days when choosing hours. The
tradition reads it cold and moist: of silver, tides, waters, growth, the common
folk. Its day Monday; ruler of Cancer.

== the-celestial-sphere | firmament ==
The celestial sphere is the oldest working picture of the sky: the stars taken as
fixed to one great globe turning about the Earth once a day, with the Earth at
its centre. It is not true — the turning is the Earth's own spin, and the stars
lie at wildly different depths — but as a map it still serves every stargazer and
navigator. On it are drawn the celestial poles (above the Earth's), the celestial
equator, and the ecliptic, the Sun's yearly path.

== the-ecliptic | the-suns-path ==
The ecliptic is the line the Sun seems to trace against the stars through the
year; the planets and Moon keep close to it, so the band around it (some eight
degrees either side) is the zodiac. It tilts about 23.5 degrees to the celestial
equator because the Earth's axis is tilted — and that tilt, not any nearness to
the Sun, is the true cause of the seasons. Where ecliptic and equator cross are
the equinoxes; their farthest parting, the solstices.

== equinox-and-solstice | the-quarters ==
The year's four hinges. At the two equinoxes (about 20 March, 23 September) the
Sun crosses the celestial equator and day and night stand near equal the world
over. At the summer solstice (about 21 June) the Sun rides highest and the day is
longest in the north; at the winter solstice (about 21 December) lowest and
shortest. They open the four seasons and, in the old reckoning, the cardinal
signs Aries, Cancer, Libra and Capricorn.

== precession | the-great-year ==
The Earth's axis slowly wheels like a spun top, one full turn in about 25,800
years — the precession of the equinoxes, found by Hipparchus about 130 BC. By it
the pole star changes over ages (Thuban once, Polaris now, Vega far hence) and
the equinox creeps backward through the signs. This is why the "tropical" signs
of astrology (tied to the seasons) have drifted from the constellations that
named them: the sign Aries no longer sits on the stars of Aries.

== the-constellations | star-figures ==
The constellations are figures the eye draws among the stars — the stars within
one are mostly unrelated, lying at different depths, and the figure is a memory-
aid, not a true group. Ptolemy listed 48 in the Almagest (about 150 AD); the
modern sky is divided into 88 agreed regions. The twelve of the zodiac are those
the ecliptic runs through. Each age and people drew its own figures on the same
stars.

== the-fixed-stars | behenian-stars ==
The "fixed" stars are those that keep their figures, against the seven that
wander — fixed only to the eye, for they too move, unseeably slow. Medieval and
Renaissance astrology singled out fifteen Behenian stars (from Arabic bahman) as
especially potent for talismans — among them Aldebaran, Regulus, Antares,
Sirius, Spica, Algol — each with a planetary nature, a stone and a plant, set
down by Agrippa after the Arabic sources. A bridge from the stars to the
talismanic art.

== sirius | dog-star | canis-major ==
Sirius, in Canis Major, the brightest star of the night sky, a near and hot white
sun (with a dim white-dwarf companion). To Egypt its dawn rising foretold the
Nile's flood and opened the year; to Greece and Rome it marked the sweltering
"dog days" of high summer. One of the Behenian stars, read as hot and Jupiter-
Mars in nature. A fixed star woven deep into calendar and lore.

== the-pleiades | seven-sisters ==
The Pleiades, a true cluster of young blue stars in Taurus — stars really born
together, unlike a constellation — seen as a little misty knot of six or seven.
Their heliacal rising and setting marked the seasons of sailing and sowing across
the ancient world, and nearly every people tells of "seven sisters." A rare case
where the eye's figure is a real family of stars.

== the-pole-star | polaris | ursa-major ==
Polaris, the North Star, stands almost still while the sky wheels about it,
because it sits near the north celestial pole — so it has steered travellers and
sailors for ages. It is found by the "Pointers" of the Plough (Ursa Major, the
Great Bear). But the pole wanders by precession: Polaris is only the present
pole-star, not an eternal one.

== the-milky-way | via-lactea ==
The Milky Way, the pale band across the night, is the massed light of countless
stars of our own galaxy seen edge-on from within — proven so by Galileo's glass
in 1610, guessed before. Myth read it as spilt milk of a goddess, or a road of
souls, or a river in the sky. The plain truth is grander than the myths: we are
looking down the length of our own star-city.

== solar-eclipse ==
A solar eclipse falls when the new Moon passes squarely between Earth and Sun and
its shadow touches the Earth, hiding the Sun for a few minutes along a narrow
track — possible only because Sun and Moon chance to look the same size in our
sky. It can happen only at new Moon, and only when that falls near a node (where
the Moon's path crosses the ecliptic), which is why it is not monthly. Long read
as an omen; in truth a clockwork of shadows, predictable far ahead.

== lunar-eclipse ==
A lunar eclipse falls when the full Moon passes into the Earth's shadow and
dims, often reddening — the "blood moon," lit only by sunlight bent through
Earth's air. It can happen only at full Moon near a node. That the Earth's shadow
on the Moon is always round was, to the Greeks, a proof the Earth is a sphere.
Like the solar sort, an omen to the old world, a reckonable shadow to the new.

== the-lunar-nodes | dragons-head-and-tail ==
The Moon's path is tilted to the ecliptic, and the two points where they cross
are the nodes — the ascending node the Dragon's Head (Caput Draconis, Rahu), the
descending the Dragon's Tail (Cauda Draconis, Ketu). Eclipses fall only near
them, whence the myth of a dragon swallowing Sun or Moon. Astrology reads the
Head as increasing and the Tail as diminishing. The nodes themselves slowly
circle the zodiac in about 18.6 years.

== the-moons-phases ==
The Moon shows phases because we see its sunlit half at changing angles as it
circles Earth: new (dark, with the Sun), waxing crescent, first quarter (half-
lit, rising at noon), waxing gibbous, full (opposite the Sun, rising at sunset),
then waning back through gibbous, last quarter and crescent to new. The round
takes about 29.5 days. The old magic timed its works to them — waxing to build
and draw, waning to banish and loose — and reckoned the Moon's "age" in days from
the new.

== comets | hairy-stars ==
A comet is a small body of ice and dust from the system's cold edge; nearing the
Sun it steams off a glowing head and a tail that always points away from the Sun,
driven by its light and wind. The Greeks named them "hairy stars" (kometes). Long
dreaded as omens of a king's death or of war, they were tamed by Halley, who in
1705 showed one returns on a fixed round of about 76 years — turning a portent
into a prediction.

== the-four-elements | the-qualities ==
Beneath the old astronomy and astrology lie the four elements of Empedocles and
Aristotle — earth, water, air, fire — built from pairings of two qualities: cold
and dry (earth), cold and moist (water), hot and moist (air), hot and dry (fire).
The signs, planets, humours and herbs are all sorted by them, which is how this
book and the herbal speak the same language. A model long overturned in natural
science, but still the grammar of the esoteric tradition.

== zodiac | zodiacus | the-twelve-signs ==
The zodiac is the band of the twelve signs the Sun and planets pass through along
the ecliptic: Aries, Taurus, Gemini, Cancer, Leo, Virgo, Libra, Scorpio,
Sagittarius, Capricorn, Aquarius, Pisces — four of fire, four of earth, four of
air, four of water, and each also cardinal, fixed or mutable. Each is the house
of one or more of the seven planets, and each governs a part of the body, head
(Aries) to feet (Pisces). Note the signs (even thirty-degree divisions tied to
the seasons) are no longer the constellations that named them; precession has
parted them.

== the-decans | faces ==
Each sign divides into three decans or "faces" of ten degrees, thirty-six in all
around the zodiac — a scheme out of Egypt, where the decans were star-groups that
rose through the night and told the hours. Later astrology gave each a planetary
ruler and the talismanic art gave each an image; in the Tarot the thirty-six
lesser cards (the twos through tens) are laid upon them. A fine grid that joins
the signs, the planets and the diviner's cards.

== the-lunar-mansions | the-twenty-eight ==
The lunar mansions are the Moon's own zodiac: twenty-eight resting-places, one
for each night of its round through the stars, known to Arabic (manazil), Indian
(nakshatra) and Chinese astronomy alike, each developed on its own. Western magic
took the Arabic twenty-eight for choosing hours and making talismans, each
mansion with its nature, its angel and its fit works. The Moon's nightly station,
as the signs are the Sun's monthly ones.
