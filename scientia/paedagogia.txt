== teaching | what-works ==
Teaching has a core of methods that hold across subjects, ages and places — found
by cognitive science, not folklore, and among the most-replicated results in the
field. The strongest few: test yourself rather than reread (retrieval practice);
spread study over time, don't cram (spacing); get prompt, specific feedback; study
worked examples before trying alone; and chase understanding, not the warm glow of
familiarity. Much of what feels like good studying (rereading, highlighting) feels
productive and mostly isn't. The hardest truth under it all: what feels easy in the
moment usually teaches least.

== retrieval-practice | the-testing-effect ==
The single best-attested finding: pulling knowledge out of memory strengthens it
far more than putting it in again. A student who reads a passage once and then
tries to recall it remembers far more, weeks later, than one who reads it four
times — though the rereader feels more confident. So the test is not just a measure
but a tool of learning. In practice: close the book and recall; use flashcards and
quizzes; ask questions, don't just re-present. "Retrieval is the event that
changes memory."

== spacing | distributed-practice ==
The same study time does more good spread out than massed. Review a thing today,
then in a few days, then in a week, then a month, and it lodges deep; cram it all in
one sitting and it fades fast after the exam. The forgetting between sessions is not
waste — the effortful re-retrieval is what builds durability. Spaced repetition
systems (Leitner boxes, Anki) automate the timing, widening the gap each time a
card is recalled. Slower to feel learned, far longer to last.

== interleaving | mixing-practice ==
Practising several related kinds of problem mixed together beats doing many of one
kind in a block — even though blocking feels smoother and more successful at the
time. Mixing forces the learner to notice which kind of problem this is and choose
the method, which is the very skill a blocked drill skips. Shown clearly in maths
and in perceptual learning (telling painters' styles apart). The cost is that it
feels harder and more error-strewn while you do it; the gain shows at the test and
in the real world, where problems don't come pre-sorted.

== worked-examples | cognitive-load ==
For a beginner, studying a fully worked-out example teaches more, per minute, than
struggling to solve the like problem unaided — because a novice's working memory is
narrow (Cognitive Load Theory, Sweller), and unguided struggle spends it all on
flailing rather than on learning the method. So: show the whole solution, step by
step, before asking them to do one. But as skill grows this reverses (the
"expertise-reversal effect") — the expert is slowed by examples they no longer
need. Hence "faded" guidance: full example, then half-done, then on their own.

== scaffolding | the-zone ==
Vygotsky's Zone of Proximal Development: the fertile band between what a learner can
do alone and what they cannot yet do at all — the things they can do only with help.
Good teaching works that band, giving support (a hint, a frame, a part-done task, a
worked step) that lets the learner reach just past their grasp, then removing it as
they steady — "scaffolding," taken down as the building stands. Too easy and nothing
is learned; too hard and they drown. Aim the help at the edge of the possible.

== feedback | the-corrective-loop ==
Learning needs a loop: attempt, find out what was right and wrong, adjust, attempt
again. Feedback is among the most powerful levers in teaching — but only when it is
specific, timely, and about the work rather than the person ("this step dropped the
sign," not "good job" or "you're clever"). Praise of ability can even harm, teaching
the learner to protect a label rather than risk a mistake. The best feedback tells
them where they are, where they're going, and the next step to close the gap.

== mastery-learning | bloom ==
Mastery learning: don't move on until the current step is truly learned, letting
time vary and holding the standard fixed, instead of the usual way round. Bloom's
famous "2 sigma" finding was that one-to-one tutoring with mastery could lift an
average student roughly two standard deviations — near the top of an ordinary class
— setting the grand problem of how to get tutoring's gains at scale. The lasting
lessons even without a tutor: check for real understanding before advancing, and let
the slow step take the time it needs.

== direct-instruction | explicit-teaching ==
For knowledge and skills that are new to the learner, explicit teaching — clear
statement, modelled example, guided practice, then independent practice, with
frequent checks — reliably outperforms leaving novices to discover the matter for
themselves. Discovery and open inquiry have their place, but mostly once a learner
has the grounding to explore with; thrown at beginners they tend to teach the
confident wrong thing. Be clear, model it, practise it, check it. Minimal guidance
fails the novice precisely when guidance is needed most.

== elaboration-and-dual-coding ==
Two ways to deepen encoding. Elaboration: make the learner explain how and why, tie
the new thing to what they already know, ask "how does this connect?" — knowledge
built into a web is far better held and retrieved than a fact left bare. Dual coding:
pair words with a fitting picture, diagram or spatial layout, so the mind stores it
two ways at once (but a decorative image that merely distracts hurts — the picture
must carry meaning). Self-explanation, concept maps and good diagrams all draw on
these.

== deliberate-practice ==
Skill grows not from mere hours but from deliberate practice (Ericsson): focused
work at the edge of your ability, on the specific weakness, with immediate feedback
and repeated correction — not the comfortable rehearsal of what you can already do.
The expert pianist drills the hard four bars, slowly, until they yield, rather than
playing the whole piece through again. Push just past the current limit, isolate the
failing part, get told at once how it went, and repeat. Pleasant practice and
effective practice are rarely the same.

== the-socratic-method ==
Teaching by question rather than by telling: the teacher draws the learner out with
a chain of well-aimed questions, exposing what they don't yet understand and leading
them to build the answer themselves. Its power is that the learner does the
cognitive work — and what you generate yourself you hold better than what you are
handed (the "generation effect"). Its risk is that it demands the learner already
have something to reason from; on bare ground it becomes guessing. Best for
sharpening, examining and deepening what is partly known.

== desirable-difficulties ==
The deep paradox under all of this (Bjork): the conditions that make learning feel
hard and slow in the moment — spacing, interleaving, testing, varying the setting —
are often the very ones that make it last; and the conditions that make practice
feel smooth and successful often produce learning that evaporates. So a learner's
own sense of how well a session went is a poor and even inverted guide. Trust the
delayed test, not the feeling of fluency. Difficulty, of the right kind and degree,
is not the enemy of learning but its engine.

== the-experts-blind-spot ==
The curse of knowledge: once you know a thing well, you cannot easily feel what it
is to not know it, so the expert skips the steps that are now invisible to them and
loses the beginner. The cure is to make the hidden steps explicit, to watch where
learners actually stumble (not where you expect), and to remember that your own
fluency is exactly what disqualifies your intuition about what is hard. The best
teacher keeps a live memory of being confused.

== motivation | why-they-try ==
No method teaches a learner who will not engage, so motivation is ground, not
garnish. The durable sort is mostly intrinsic, and grows from three things
(self-determination theory): competence (a felt sense of getting better — which
early, well-pitched success feeds), autonomy (some real ownership of the how and
what), and relatedness (that someone cares how they do). A "growth mindset" — taking
ability as trainable, so effort and error are the path and not a verdict — supports
all three. Reward and fear can drive the short sprint, but tend to crowd out the
longer love of the thing.
