== the-sworn-book | liber-iuratus | liber-sacer | sworn-book-of-honorius ==
The Sworn Book of Honorius (Liber Iuratus Honorii) is one of the oldest and
weightiest texts of medieval ritual magic. Its Latin manuscripts call it the
Sacred or Sworn Book, and once the Book of the Angels. It is not a heap of
separate spells but one coherent method: a devout, liturgical magic whose
crown is the vision of God, with the same frame then turned to angels and
spirits for lesser ends. Hedegard, who edited the Latin in 2002, dates it
between 1100 and 1350; scholars argue between the early 13th century (if
William of Auvergne's "Liber Sacratus," c. 1230, is this book) and the early
14th (if its prologue answers Pope John XXII, 1316-1334). Few manuscripts
survive and only one is complete; the oldest, Sloane 3854, is 14th-century.
Source: Peterson's edition and translation (2016), the first complete one.

== the-prologue | the-council-of-masters ==
The book's own tale of its birth, and best read as legend. Evil spirits stir
the Pope and cardinals to condemn magic and its masters to death. The masters,
who say no impure man can work the art and that spirits are forced to answer
the pure, call a general council: 89 masters from Naples, Athens and Toledo
choose one, Honorius son of Euclid, master of Thebes, to save the art. With
the angel Hocrohel advising, he draws on seven books of magic and distils 93
chapters into one short book, called Sacred because it holds the hundred
sacred names of God. Peterson notes the tale "smacks of fiction"; it is
missing from the older form of the text kept by Berengar Ganell, and so may
be a later addition.

== the-oath-of-honorius | the-nine-oaths ==
Why the book is called Sworn. Whoever receives it swears: that it be given to
no one until its master is at the point of death; that no more than three
copies be made; that it go to no woman, and to no man unless mature, upright
and faithful, proved by a full year's watching; that it never be destroyed,
but returned to the master or his heirs; that if no fit heir is found it be
buried in the master's tomb or in a clean, honourable and secret place; that
disciples be ready to die for the teaching; that they neither pry into their
master's doings nor reveal that he knows such things; that the master bind
his disciples as a father his sons, in concord, each bearing the others'
suffering and keeping their secrets; and last, that each who takes the oath
lay it on the next. This is the book's own claim, in its prologue.

== honorius-of-thebes | the-theban-alphabet ==
Honorius, "son of Euclid, master of Thebes," is held by scholars to be a
pseudonym; the Thebes meant is probably the Greek city, not the Egyptian.
Outside the Sworn Book the only early notice of him is Trithemius, whose
Polygraphia (written 1508, printed 1518) gives a cipher alphabet of "Honorius
surnamed the Theban," citing Peter of Abano for it; Peterson could not find
the passage in Abano. The alphabet is built on the Latin letters, not the
Greek. It reached the wider world through Agrippa (De Occulta Philosophia,
1533), and the one Honorius manuscript that has it copied it from Agrippa.
Its use as a "witches' alphabet" in Wicca and modern magic is modern.

== the-seal-of-god-in-honorius | sigillum-dei-honorii ==
The Seal of God (Sigillum Dei), which the Sworn Book also calls the Seal of
Solomon, is the heart of its apparatus and far better known than the book
itself: its making opens the first Book, and the vision of God is sought by
it. It belongs to a tradition that spread from the Mediterranean across
Europe; it is found on metal, in manuscripts, and in at least one printed Key
of Solomon. Later copies drift far from the oldest (the figure in La
Veritable Magie Noire, 1750, is barely recognisable), and John Dee's Sigillum
Dei Aemeth descends from it. Seven divine names used in the seal appear to be
taken more or less directly from the Jewish Book of Raziel.

== the-beatific-vision | the-divine-vision ==
The centrepiece of the Sworn Book is a rite to gain the beatific vision: to
see God while still living. The first Book lays it out in order: the making
of the Seal of God, the vision itself, a first and a second purification, the
placating of the divine majesty, a separation, the names of the living God,
and the completion of the work. Its means are those of strict Christian
devotion bent to a magical end: set prayers, the liturgical hours,
abstinence, a hair shirt, and the help of a priest and his masses. The book
teaches direct knowledge of God, not through the Church, which is why it
stood so sharply against the authorities of its day.

== the-five-books-of-honorius | the-works-of-honorius ==
How the Sworn Book is laid out in Peterson's edition. Book I: preparing the
Seal of God and the divine vision. Book II: the angels, the natures and
offices of the planetary angels, and the circle and rites to call and bind
them. Book III: the spirits of the air, their nature, the raising of the
winds, the circle, seals and bonds, and their placating. Book IV: the spirits
of the earth and how they are called. Book V: a general exposition, with the
consecration of the ink, the masses, and the making of a magic whistle. A
long list of ends to be gained (to know the heavens and the angels' names,
seals and offices, the hour of death, things past, present and future, the
virtues of planets, herbs and beasts) stands at the front; it looks like a
table of contents but is not one.

== the-name-of-seventy-two-letters | the-great-name ==
The first topic of the Sworn Book's first work is the composing of the great
name of God, "which the Hebrews call Shem ha-Meforash," of 72 letters, named
there as the beginning of the art. Honorius keeps the Jewish term but his
details differ from the Kabbalists' (as from Abulafia, who wrote on the
72-letter name and taught in Greece, Thebes among other places, in the
1270s). The book is also said to hold a hundred sacred names of God, whence
"Sacred."

== the-kings-of-the-air | the-planetary-kings ==
In the Sworn Book the spirits of the air are ranked by the seven planets,
each band under a king, with servants below and ruling angels above who
constrain them. The kings as Honorius names them: Maymon for Saturn, Formione
for Jupiter, Iammax for Mars, Barthan for the Sun, Sarabocres for Venus,
Habaa for Mercury, Harthan for the Moon. Near kin of these lists stand in the
Heptameron ascribed to Peter of Abano, in a Byzantine manual of exorcism, in
the 15th-century Book of Angels, and (for the Moon) in the Astromagia of
Alfonso X, c. 1280; the spellings shift from book to book. Hedegard and
Boudet suspect a Greek source behind the planetary chapters.

== the-roots-of-honorius | honorius-and-his-sources ==
What the Sworn Book is made of, as scholars read it. Jewish: union with God
by magical means, the powers of the seven planets, the divine names, visions
in sleep, the heavenly palace of the Hekhalot writings, the word Shamayim for
heaven, and likeness to the Book of Raziel. Western Christian: the Apostles'
and Athanasian creeds, the Roman blessing of salt, and the nine orders of
angels, though in its own order, with Cherubim set before Seraphim and
Principalities before Powers. Eastern: some knowledge of the Greek Orthodox
liturgy. Honorius claims Solomon's authority, yet little of his method comes
from the Key of Solomon or its kin; prayers from the Ars Notoria are there,
but were added in a later revision.

== the-fame-of-honorius | honorius-in-history ==
The Sworn Book was notorious long before it was read. Notices of it, some
only probable: William of Auvergne c. 1230; the trial of Olivier Pepin at
Mende c. 1347; the inquisitor Eymerich in 1376; John Gower's Confessio
Amantis in 1397; Trithemius in 1518; John Dee's secret writings in 1582.
A. E. Waite (1898) made the English title current but described only the
prologue; Thorndike surveyed it; Driscoll printed a partial, altered version
in 1977 in 450 copies. Hedegard's critical Latin text came in 2002, and the
older version quoted at length in Berengar Ganell's Summa Sacre Magice has
since reshaped the study of it. Owen Davies ranks Honorius second only to
Solomon in medieval notoriety.
