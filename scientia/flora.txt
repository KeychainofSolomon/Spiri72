== barley | hordeum | bere ==
Barley (Hordeum vulgare), a cereal grass, one of the first grains farmed in the
Near East. Nourishing and easy on the body: boiled to a water (ptisan) it was
the classic sick-room drink for fevers and the chest, and sprouted it becomes
malt, the ground of ale and beer. Galen reckoned it cold and dry, cooling where
wheat warms; Culpeper set it under Saturn. A plain, foundational food-grain
before it is any kind of physic.

== wheat | triticum | frumentum ==
Wheat (Triticum), the chief bread-grain of the West, richer and more warming
than barley. The flour binds and nourishes; a poultice of bread and milk draws
and softens swellings. The humoral writers call it temperate, warm and moist,
and Culpeper gives it to the Sun or Venus. In the older symbol-reading it is the
grain of Ceres and of resurrection, buried to rise.

== oats | avena | groats ==
Oats (Avena sativa), a hardy northern grain for porridge and horse-feed. A warm
oat poultice eases aches; an oat bath soothes itchy and inflamed skin (the real
virtue is in the starch and saponins). Gentle and sustaining. The tradition
reads it temperate; modern use keeps it as a calming food for the nerves and
the gut, which the old herbals only hint at.

== garlic | allium | poor-mans-treacle ==
Garlic (Allium sativum). Hot and dry in the fourth (highest) degree by the old
reckoning, and pungent for real reason: its cut cloves make allicin, with true
antibacterial force. Long eaten against plague and worms and to thin the blood,
and hung against the evil eye and, late and in fiction, the vampire. Culpeper
set it under Mars for its heat and fire. "Poor man's treacle" — the commoner's
cure-all.

== rosemary | rosmarinus | dew-of-the-sea ==
Rosemary (Salvia rosmarinus), an aromatic evergreen of the Mediterranean. Its
oil is stimulating and sharpens the mind in more than fancy; "rosemary for
remembrance" (Ophelia) rests on an old and not baseless link to memory and
alertness. Burned to cleanse a sick room, carried at weddings and funerals
alike. Culpeper gives it to the Sun and to Aries; a herb of memory, fidelity and
warding.

== sage | salvia | wise-herb ==
Sage (Salvia officinalis). Its very name (salvere, to save) carries its
reputation: "why should a man die while sage grows in his garden?" An astringent,
drying herb, good as a gargle for the mouth and throat (a true mild antiseptic).
Culpeper sets it under Jupiter. Burned as a cleansing smoke — though the
"smudging" of dried white sage is a Native American rite, borrowed late into
Western practice, not an old European one.

== thyme | thymus | running-thyme ==
Thyme (Thymus vulgaris). A warm, drying kitchen herb whose oil (thymol) is a
genuine antiseptic, once used in salves and to keep meat. Reckoned hot and dry,
and set by Culpeper under Venus. Laid among linen against moth, and in folk lore
a herb where the fae were said to gather; a sprig was thought to give courage.

== basil | basilicum | ocimum ==
Basil (Ocimum basilicum). A tender, sweet-scented kitchen herb of warm reading.
Its name is tangled: tied by some to basileus (king, the "royal herb") and by
others, through false etymology, to the basilisk, so the old writers quarrelled
over whether it was wholesome or venomous. Culpeper, no friend to it, set it
under Mars and Scorpio. In Italy a token of love; in India (holy basil, tulsi) a
sacred plant, a different species and tradition.

== mint | mentha | garden-mint ==
Mint (Mentha), cooling to the taste yet reckoned warm and dry in use; the menthol
is a true carminative, settling the stomach and the wind. Strewn for its scent,
chewed to sweeten the breath. Culpeper sets it under Venus. Named for the nymph
Minthe of Greek myth, trodden into the herb by a jealous goddess.

== chamomile | chamomilla | maythen ==
Chamomile (Matricaria / Chamaemelum), a small daisy-flowered herb. Its tea is a
mild and genuine calmative and settles the stomach; a wash soothes the skin.
"The more it is trodden the faster it grows," said as a figure for patience.
Culpeper set it under the Sun, and the Saxons counted it among their nine sacred
herbs. Gentle enough for children, which few strong simples are.

== betony | betonica | bishopwort | woundwort ==
Betony (Betonica officinalis, formerly Stachys). A famous all-heal of the old
world: Antonius Musa wrote a whole treatise of its virtues, and the saying ran
"sell your coat and buy betony." Taken for the head — head-ache, and "frightful
dreams" — and worn as a ward against ill spirits and the night. Culpeper set it
under Jupiter and Aries. More renown than proven power, but a true nervine and
bitter.

== vervain | verbena | holy-herb | herba-sacra ==
Vervain (Verbena officinalis). The Romans' herba sacra, swept the altars of
Jupiter and carried by the heralds (fetiales) who swore treaties — a herb of
oaths, peace and cleansing. The Druids are said (by Pliny) to have gathered it
with rite. Used in washes for the mouth and sores. Culpeper set it under Venus.
Esteemed in rite and ward far beyond its modest medicine.

== rue | ruta | herb-of-grace ==
Rue (Ruta graveolens). Bitter, pungent and reckoned hot and dry in the third
degree. A little was held to sharpen the sight and quicken the wit and to cool
unchaste heat; it was strewn against plague and the evil eye, and brushes for
holy water were bound of it — "herb of grace." But it is no trifle: in quantity
it burns and sickens, raises blisters in sun on the skin, and is a known
abortifacient, so it was forbidden to women with child. Culpeper gave it to the
Sun and Leo.

== mugwort | artemisia-vulgaris | cronewort | felon-herb | sailors-tobacco ==
Mugwort (Artemisia vulgaris) — "mater herbarum," the mother of herbs, named for
Artemis. (Not motherwort, which is the quite different Leonurus cardiaca.) A
traveller's herb, laid in the shoe against weariness, and a woman's herb for the
courses, being a true emmenagogue. Hung in the house and burned as a smoke to
cleanse, and famed as a dream herb, laid under the pillow for clear and telling
dreams. Culpeper set it under Venus; one of the Saxon nine herbs.

== wormwood | absinthium | green-ginger ==
Wormwood (Artemisia absinthium) — kin to mugwort but its own plant; the bare name
"artemisia" fits the whole clan, not this one alone. Among the bitterest of
herbs: it strengthens a weak stomach, stirs the appetite, and was steeped in wine
against worms and low spirits (and gives absinthe its name and bite). Culpeper
set it under Mars, and reckoned it hot and dry in the second degree (sources
differ on the exact degree). A little serves; too much offends the head.

== yarrow | achillea | milfoil | nosebleed ==
Yarrow (Achillea millefolium), named for Achilles, who was said to staunch his
soldiers' wounds with it — and it does stem bleeding, a real styptic (hence
"nosebleed" and "soldier's woundwort"). Also drunk as a sweating tea for fevers.
Culpeper set it under Venus. In China its dried stalks were the proper lots for
casting the I Ching, a thread that joins this herbal to the diviner's art.

== st-johns-wort | hypericum | fuga-daemonum ==
St John's wort (Hypericum perforatum). Gathered at Midsummer (St John's eve),
its leaves pin-pricked with light where the oil-glands sit, yielding a blood-red
oil — a true vulnerary salve for wounds and burns, and now known to lift low
mood (hypericin), though it crosses many medicines. Hung in windows and worn as
"fuga daemonum," the devils'-flight, against storm, lightning and ill spirits.
Culpeper set it under the Sun and Leo, a solar, protective herb.

== valerian | valeriana | all-heal | setwall ==
Valerian (Valeriana officinalis). Its unlovely-smelling root is a genuine
sedative and sleep-bringer, long taken for the nerves and for wakeful nights
(and much loved by cats, as catnip is). Reckoned warm; Culpeper set it under
Mercury. A plain, working physic of the calming sort, with little myth attached —
which is itself a mark of authenticity.

== elder | sambucus | elder-tree | lady-ellhorn ==
Elder (Sambucus nigra). A whole apothecary in one tree: the flowers make a
sweating tea for colds and a soothing water for the skin, the ripe berries a
cordial for the same (both must be cooked — raw berries, bark and leaf are
mildly toxic). Hollow stems became pipes and bellows. Hedged about with awe: the
"Elder Mother" (Hylde-Moer) was said to dwell in it, and you asked her leave
before cutting. Culpeper set it under Venus.

== mistletoe | viscum | all-heal | golden-bough ==
Mistletoe (Viscum album), a parasite that never touches earth, green in dead
winter — which is the root of its wonder. Pliny tells of the Druids cutting the
oak-mistletoe with a golden sickle; it is Aeneas's Golden Bough and the dart that
slew Norse Baldr. Hung for the midwinter kiss. But it is poisonous taken
inwardly, not a food; its medicine is for careful hands only. Culpeper gave it to
the Sun. Esteemed far more in rite than in the pot.

== nettle | urtica | stinging-nettle ==
Nettle (Urtica dioica). Its sting is real chemistry (formic acid and histamine),
yet cooked or dried it is a wholesome pot-herb rich in iron, and its fibre was
spun to cloth. Taken as a spring tonic to "cleanse the blood," and the old (and
genuine) trick of urtication — stinging aching joints on purpose — to draw heat.
Culpeper set it under Mars, for its fire.

== plantain | plantago | waybread | englishmans-foot ==
Plantain (Plantago major), the low rosette of every path and waste. A true
first-aid leaf: crushed on a sting, cut or nettle-rash it soothes and staunches,
and the seed (psyllium) is a gentle bulking laxative. One of the Saxon nine
sacred herbs ("Waybread, mother of worts"). Culpeper set it under Venus. Humble,
everywhere, and it works.

== mandrake | mandragora ==
Mandrake (Mandragora officinarum), a true narcotic of the nightshade kin,
reckoned cold in the third degree. Its forked root was likened to a human body
and hedged with the tale of its deadly scream when drawn. Used sparingly as a
sleep- and pain-bringer before the knife (one of the old soporifics), and in any
excess a stupefying poison. A strong simple, never a food — handle as such.

== henbane | hyoscyamus | stinking-nightshade ==
Henbane (Hyoscyamus niger). A baneful nightshade, deadly poisonous: its tropane
alkaloids (hyoscyamine, scopolamine) are the real engine of both its old,
dangerous use as a pain-killer and sleep-draught and its place in witch-lore and
the "flying ointments." Culpeper set it, rightly, under Saturn, the cold and
deathly planet. Named and known as a poison first; no part of it is safe to eat
or brew. Described here as the herbals describe it — as a danger to be known, not
a recipe.

== foxglove | digitalis | deadmans-bells ==
Foxglove (Digitalis purpurea), tall spires of thimble flowers. Beautiful and
deadly: it holds cardiac glycosides that William Withering, in 1785, learned to
use in exact doses for dropsy and the failing heart — the origin of the real
heart-drug digitalis. But the line between remedy and poison is a knife-edge, so
it is never a home simple. Its folk names (dead man's bells, witches' gloves)
keep the warning. Under Venus by some, Saturn by its danger.
