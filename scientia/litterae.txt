== writing | the-craft ==
Good prose is made, not found, and mostly in the rewriting. A few principles hold
across kinds: know what one thing the piece is about and cut what does not serve it;
prefer the concrete to the abstract, the short word to the long, the active to the
passive where it is clearer; show the reader the thing rather than naming the
feeling; and read it aloud, for the ear catches what the eye forgives. The first
draft is for getting it down; the craft is in the second and the sixth.

== narrative-structure ==
Most stories answer the same under-shape: a person wants something, is kept from
it, struggles, and is changed. The Western workhorse is the three acts — setup
(who, where, the disturbance), confrontation (rising trouble to a crisis), and
resolution. Freytag's pyramid names the beats: exposition, rising action, climax,
falling action, denouement. These are scaffolds, not laws; a story that has stakes,
escalation and consequence will feel shaped whether or not you counted acts.

== story-shapes | the-models ==
Several templates, each a lens, none a rule. The Hero's Journey (Campbell, by way
of Vogler) — the call, the threshold, trials, the ordeal, the return with the boon
— fits myth and much adventure, and can feel formulaic if followed by rote. The
older four- or five-act shapes suit tragedy and the stage. East Asian storytelling
often uses kishōtenketsu, a four-part shape (introduction, development, a turn or
unexpected twist, reconciliation) that needs no conflict at all — a useful reminder
that struggle is one engine of story, not the only one.

== point-of-view ==
Who tells it, and how near. First person ("I") is intimate and partial — we know
only what the narrator knows, and may doubt them. Third limited ("she") rides one
character's shoulder, the modern default, close but outside. Third omniscient knows
all minds and ages, grand but distancing. Second ("you") is rare and insistent.
The key discipline is consistency of distance and knowledge: don't give the
narrator sight they couldn't have, and don't slip heads mid-scene without meaning
to ("head-hopping"). Point of view is the reader's whole access to the world.

== show-dont-tell ==
The most-quoted writing advice, and the most abused. To "show" is to render — the
clenched jaw, the untouched plate — and let the reader infer the anger; to "tell"
is to state it: "she was angry." Showing immerses and trusts the reader; it is
right for the moments that matter. But telling is faster, and a story that shows
everything drowns — summary and plain statement are how you cross the dull stretches
and control pace. The skill is not to show always, but to show what counts and tell
the rest.

== character ==
Readers follow people, not plots. A character lives by wanting something (a visible
goal) and needing something (a deeper lack they often can't see), and by the gap
between the two. Give them contradiction, a specific voice, and a past that presses
on the present; reveal them through choice under pressure, which is where character
actually shows. Flat characters (one note) are fine for the edges; the ones we
follow must be round enough to surprise us convincingly. Change — or the pointed
refusal to change — is the arc.

== dialogue ==
Good dialogue is not how people really talk (which is dull, looping and full of
"um") but a sharpened illusion of it. It should do more than one job at once —
carry information, reveal character, raise tension — and lean on subtext, people
saying one thing and meaning another, since on-the-nose talk ("I am angry at you,
my brother") rings false. Trust "said"; it disappears. Cut the greetings and the
small talk. Let what is not said do work. Each speaker should sound unlike the
others with the tags removed.

== prose-style ==
Style is the how of the saying, and clarity is its first virtue. Orwell's rules
still bite: cut needless words, avoid the stale metaphor and the long word where a
short serves, prefer the active, and break any of these sooner than write something
barbarous. Vary sentence length for rhythm — a run of equal sentences drones; a
short one after long ones lands. But style serves voice and matter: the plain style
is not the only good one, and a baroque sentence, controlled, can do what a plain
one cannot. Know the rule before you break it.

== revision | editing ==
Writing is rewriting. Draft to discover, then revise in passes, coarse to fine:
first structure (does the whole hold, is anything missing or out of order), then
scene and paragraph (does each earn its place), last line and word (cut, sharpen,
fix). "Kill your darlings" — the clever bit that serves only itself must go. Read
aloud; give it time cold so you can see it as a stranger would; and learn your own
tics to hunt them. Most of the quality a reader feels was put in after the first
draft, not during it.

== the-prose-forms ==
Prose fiction sorts roughly by length and ambition. The novel (tens of thousands of
words up) has room for many threads and a world; the novella is a single powerful
line pursued; the short story turns on one change or revelation, every word
counting; flash fiction does it in a page. In non-fiction, the essay tries a thought
(from Montaigne's essayer, "to attempt") — the personal essay through a self, the
argumentative toward a claim. Each form has its physics: what a novel can meander
toward, a short story must imply.

== rhetoric | the-art-of-persuasion ==
Rhetoric is the ancient art of effective speech and writing, and its bones still
hold. Aristotle's three appeals: ethos (the speaker's credibility and character),
pathos (the audience's feeling), logos (the argument's reason) — persuasion
usually needs all three. The classical parts order a case: exordium (open, win a
hearing), narratio (set out the facts), confirmatio (prove), refutatio (answer the
other side), peroratio (close and move). Not trickery in itself — the same craft
serves truth or lies; the ethics are in the use.

== rhetorical-devices ==
The named figures of forceful language, worth knowing by sight. Of repetition:
anaphora (begin successive clauses alike — "we shall fight..."), tricolon (three in
a row, with force on the third), antithesis (balanced opposites — "not this, but
that"). Of sound: alliteration, assonance. Of sense: metaphor and simile, metonymy
(naming by association — "the crown" for the king), synecdoche (part for whole),
irony, hyperbole, understatement (litotes). Chiasmus crosses a structure back on
itself ("ask not what your country..."). Tools, not ornaments — each does a
particular work on the reader.

== narrative-devices ==
The storyteller's techniques of arrangement and effect. In medias res: open in the
thick of things, fill the backstory later. Foreshadowing: plant what will pay off,
so the turn feels earned, not arbitrary ("Chekhov's gun" — a rifle shown must be
fired). The unreliable narrator: a teller we come to doubt, so the reading becomes
detective work. Frame story: a tale within a tale (the Decameron, the Thousand and
One Nights). Flashback, foreshadow, the withheld fact, dramatic irony (the reader
knows what the character does not) — all control what the reader knows, and when.

== theme ==
Theme is what the story is about under what happens — its argument about the world,
rarely stated and better not. It is not a moral stamped on the end but a question the
whole work explores by putting characters under pressure and watching what their
choices cost. Theme emerges from the concrete; a story that sets out to "say"
something tends to preach, while one that honestly follows its people to the end
says more. Let it arise; then, in revision, prune what fights it and deepen what
feeds it.
